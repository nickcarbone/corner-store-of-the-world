21 supermarkets, corner stores, kiosks and co-ops that run daily life in their countries — what to grab, how they got there, and what they mean to the people who shop there every day.

Why a grocery store tells you more than a monument

Every country has a store its people would name first if you asked where they buy milk. In Spain it is a supermarket with a cult own-brand. In Mexico it is a 24-hour corner shop where you also pay the electricity bill. In Norway it is a railway newsstand that learned to sell hot dogs. The format changes from place to place, and that change is the point: it shows whether people do one big weekly shop or five small ones, whether they drive or walk, and whether the store doubles as a bank, a post office or a political argument.

Each entry here is a local institution, picked because it has a story worth telling rather than because it is large. We bent the strictly-local rule once, for Thailand’s 7-Eleven, because a Thai conglomerate has run it since 1987 and made it something unmistakably Thai.

A note on Japan

Japan’s konbini are the world benchmark for the convenience store, and so thoroughly covered already that we left them out. Treat them as the yardstick every entry here is quietly measured against.

  • Grab this is what locals would tell you to buy, or do, there.

Method

Entries were chosen for local identity and story, not size. Each was checked against company filings or official histories, national press and trade publications; sources sit under every card. Store counts are the most recent published figures as of September 2026 and are dated on each card.

Where sources disagreed we say so or use the more conservative figure. Where a claim is contested — the Woolworths bag in a parliamentary inquiry, the Esselunga–Coop court fight — we describe it as a claim, not a finding.
